Air law, air traffic rules and procedures

  1. A crew plans to finish a roof inspection before night begins. Under the CARs definition, when does night begin?

    • A. At the start of evening civil twilight
    • B. Thirty minutes after sunset
    • C. At the end of evening civil twilight
    • D. One hour after sunset
    Show the answer

    Answer: At the end of evening civil twilight

    Why: CARs 101.01 defines night as the time between the end of evening civil twilight and the beginning of morning civil twilight, so night begins when evening civil twilight ends. The start of evening civil twilight is not the point the definition names; night begins at its end. Thirty minutes after sunset is a fixed offset that the definition does not use. One hour after sunset is likewise a fixed offset that does not appear in the definition.

    Reference: CARs 101.01(1) night · Level: Basic and Advanced

  2. A pilot's drone has an operating weight of 238 g as normally flown. Which change would require the drone to be registered before its next flight?

    • A. Flying it for pay to photograph a client's farm buildings
    • B. Adding a 9 g anti-collision strobe light for a flight at dusk
    • C. Clipping on 18 g propeller guards for work close to trees
    • D. Flying it inside controlled airspace near a city airport
    Show the answer

    Answer: Clipping on 18 g propeller guards for work close to trees

    Why: Operating weight includes any payload and any safety equipment, so 238 g plus 18 g of propeller guards is 256 g, and 900.13 requires registration from 250 g (0.55 pounds). Being paid for the flight does not bring a sub-250 g aircraft under the registration rule. A 9 g strobe brings it to 247 g, still under 250 g. Airspace does not change the registration threshold.

    Reference: CARs 900.13(1), 900.01 (operating weight) · Level: Basic and Advanced

  3. A crew's visual observer finished a glass of wine at 23:30. The flight is planned for 09:00 the next morning. Under Part IX, what is the earliest time this observer may act as a crew member?

    • A. 07:30 the next morning
    • B. 09:00 the next morning
    • C. 23:30 the next evening
    • D. 11:30 the next morning
    Show the answer

    Answer: 11:30 the next morning

    Why: 901.19(2)(a) bars any person from acting as a crew member within 12 hours after consuming an alcoholic beverage, and a visual observer is a crew member, so 23:30 plus 12 hours gives 11:30 the next morning. 07:30 allows only 8 hours. 09:00 treats the rule as if it applied to the pilot alone, so the observer could start with the flight. 23:30 the next evening applies 24 hours, which is longer than the rule sets.

    Reference: CARs 901.19(2), 901.19(2)(a), 900.01 (visual observer) · Level: Basic and Advanced

  4. During a basic operation, a pilot flies a small RPA at 300 ft (91 m) AGL. A jogger who is not involved in the operation runs along a path directly below the planned route. What does 901.26 require?

    • A. It has to stay at least 100 ft (30 m) from the jogger, measured horizontally
    • B. Its 300 ft (91 m) height above the jogger already meets the distance rule
    • C. It has to stay at least 500 ft (152.4 m) from the jogger, measured horizontally
    • D. It has to stay at least 16.4 ft (5 m) from the jogger, measured horizontally
    Show the answer

    Answer: It has to stay at least 100 ft (30 m) from the jogger, measured horizontally

    Why: 901.26(a) requires a small RPA in a VLOS operation to stay at least 100 feet (30 m) from any person not involved, measured horizontally and at any altitude, so flying 300 ft above the jogger does not satisfy it. 500 ft (152.4 m) is the 901.26(b) figure for a medium RPA outside Division V, not a small-RPA figure. 16.4 ft (5 m) is a boundary used in the advanced-operations rules (901.62(a)).

    Reference: CARs 901.26(a), 901.26(b), 901.62(a)(ii) · Level: Basic and Advanced

  5. A crew plans a VLOS operation of a small RPA at night. What lighting does 901.39 require on the aircraft?

    • A. White anti-collision lights flashing 40 to 100 times per minute
    • B. Lights, switched on, that make it visible to the pilot or observer
    • C. Anti-collision lights visible from at least 3 miles away
    • D. No lights, provided the crew watches it through night-vision goggles
    Show the answer

    Answer: Lights, switched on, that make it visible to the pilot or observer

    Why: 901.39(1) requires the RPA to carry lights that are turned on and are sufficient to make it visible to the pilot or a visual observer, with or without night-vision goggles. White anti-collision lights flashing 40 to 100 cycles per minute are the 901.38.1 standard for BVLOS operations. 901.39 sets no colour, flash rate or visibility distance for the lights. Night-vision goggles do not remove the need for lights.

    Reference: CARs 901.39(1), 901.38.1(1), 901.38.1(2)(c) · Level: Basic and Advanced

  6. A Basic certificate holder plans a VLOS flight at a site 2.5 NM from the centre of a certified airport and 1.5 NM from the centre of a certified heliport. Which distance rules out the flight as a basic operation?

    • A. The heliport distance, but not the airport distance
    • B. Both the airport distance and the heliport distance
    • C. Neither distance; both are outside the basic limits
    • D. The airport distance, but not the heliport distance
    Show the answer

    Answer: The airport distance, but not the heliport distance

    Why: 901.47(2) bars a VLOS operation less than three nautical miles from the centre of an airport or one nautical mile from the centre of a heliport, and flights inside those distances fall under the advanced-operations rules (901.62, 901.73). At 2.5 NM the site is inside the airport limit, while 1.5 NM is outside the 1 NM heliport limit.

    Reference: CARs 901.47(2), 901.47(2)(a), 901.47(2)(b), 901.62(a)(iv) · Level: Basic and Advanced

  7. An advanced pilot needs to survey farmland at 600 ft AGL, far from any building, inside controlled airspace. Under Division V, which of these can allow this height without a special flight operations certificate?

    • A. An ATS authorization for that altitude
    • B. A model declared for controlled airspace
    • C. A NOTAM issued for the survey area
    • D. Notice given to the nearest airport
    Show the answer

    Answer: An ATS authorization for that altitude

    Why: Despite the 400 feet (122 m) AGL limit of 901.25, a pilot may operate in controlled airspace under Division V above those altitudes if an authorization to that effect has been issued by the ATS provider (901.71(2)); the SFOC rules treat flight above 901.25 altitudes as a medium-complexity SFOC case only when it is not authorized under 901.71(2) (903.02(3)(d)). A controlled-airspace declaration (901.69(a)) is an equipment requirement and says nothing about altitude. A NOTAM is not the authorization that 901.71(2) names. Notifying an airport is not an approval in the rule.

    Reference: CARs 901.71(2), 903.02(3)(d) · Level: Advanced

  8. A client wants aerial mapping over land that lies inside Class F Special Use Restricted airspace. Under CARs 601.04, whose authorization does the RPA pilot need to operate there?

    • A. The person specified for that purpose in the Designated Airspace Handbook
    • B. The ATC unit listed for that airspace in the Canada Flight Supplement
    • C. Transport Canada, through a special flight operations certificate — RPAS
    • D. The owner of the land that lies under the restricted airspace
    Show the answer

    Answer: The person specified for that purpose in the Designated Airspace Handbook

    Why: CARs 601.04(2) states that no person shall operate an aircraft in Class F Special Use Restricted airspace unless authorized to do so by the person specified for that purpose in the Designated Airspace Handbook. An ATC unit listed in the Canada Flight Supplement is not the person 601.04(2) names; that person is specified in the Designated Airspace Handbook. A special flight operations certificate is not the authorization 601.04(2) names either. A landowner has no power under 601.04 to authorize flight in the airspace.

    Reference: CARs 601.04(2) · Level: Advanced

RPA airframes, power plants, propulsion and systems

  1. A crew finishes a morning survey, loads the RPA into a truck and drives to a second site for an afternoon job. Following the TC AIM, which inspection should come before the first flight at the new site?

    • A. A full inspection of the aircraft and station
    • B. A brief inspection, as after a battery swap
    • C. No new inspection; the morning one still holds
    • D. A brief check of parts that moved in transit
    Show the answer

    Answer: A full inspection of the aircraft and station

    Why: TC AIM RPA 3.2.12.1 says a brief inspection should follow each landing (for example a battery change) and a full inspection should follow each crash or malfunction, or a change of location. A brief inspection is the after-landing standard, not the one for a new site. The morning inspection is the initial one for the day; moving to a new location calls for a full inspection. A check limited to parts that moved in transit is still a brief inspection, not the full one.

    Reference: TC AIM RPA 3.2.12.1 · Level: Basic and Advanced

  2. A small RPA is drifting out of control over a crowded beach. Its parachute flight termination system, if fired now, would bring it down among the people below. Under CARs 901.44, what applies?

    • A. Activate it now, as ending a fly-away comes first
    • B. Do not activate it while it will endanger people
    • C. Do not activate it until the ANSP has been told
    • D. Activate it, as a parachute descent can't injure
    Show the answer

    Answer: Do not activate it while it will endanger people

    Why: CARs 901.44 bars activating a system that terminates the flight of an RPA if it will endanger or will likely endanger aviation safety or the safety of any person, and TC AIM RPA 3.2.32 repeats that initiation may only be done if it does not endanger anyone. Ending the fly-away does not override that bar. Telling the ANSP is part of flyaway action, but 901.44 makes the safety of persons, not an ANSP call, the condition. 901.44 makes no exception for parachute systems.

    Reference: CARs 901.44; TC AIM RPA 3.2.32, RPA 3.2.11.2 (Flyaway) · Level: Basic and Advanced

  3. The manufacturer's instructions for an RPAS say its downward positioning sensor must work for flight. Before take-off the app reports that sensor as failed, while GPS works normally. Under CARs 901.29, may the pilot launch?

    • A. Yes, by switching the flight to GPS mode
    • B. No, until a TC inspector clears the fault
    • C. Yes, once the fault is entered in the log
    • D. No, until that unit is serviceable again
    Show the answer

    Answer: No, until that unit is serviceable again

    Why: CARs 901.29(c) bars take-off unless the pilot ensures that all equipment required by the Regulations or the manufacturer's instructions, including any system element needed to support the operation, is installed and serviceable; the manual makes this sensor required equipment. Switching to GPS mode does not make a required item optional. A log entry records the fault but does not make the sensor serviceable. 901.29 puts the duty on the pilot; it does not call for a TC inspector's clearance.

    Reference: CARs 901.29(c), 901.29 · Level: Basic and Advanced

Human factors

  1. At dusk, a visual observer detects a faint light that disappears whenever she views it directly. What does the TC AIM advise?

    • A. Stare at the spot until the eyes adjust to the dimness
    • B. Close one eye and keep looking straight at the spot
    • C. Turn the tablet to full brightness, then look back
    • D. Look a little to one side of where the object was
    Show the answer

    Answer: Look a little to one side of where the object was

    Why: The TC AIM says the part of the eye best suited for night vision is not in the centre, so an object detected in barely adequate light disappears when viewed directly but will often reappear if one looks 10 to 15° to one side of it. Staring straight at the spot keeps it in the centre of the eye, which is the problem. Closing one eye may preserve some of the eyes' adjustment to low light, but looking straight at the spot still uses the centre of the eye. A bright screen works against the eyes: the TC AIM says light adaptation can be destroyed in seconds and that lights should be kept low at night to keep the dark adaptation needed to see outside.

    Reference: TC AIM AIR 3.5, RPA 3.2.6.2 · Level: Basic and Advanced

  2. On a −25 °C day, a pilot who is not dressed for the cold plans a long series of short flights. Which effect does the TC AIM describe?

    • A. Cold sharpens her alertness and offsets her fatigue
    • B. Cold affects the drone's battery but not her own skills
    • C. Cold stress takes the place of fatigue as the main concern
    • D. Her dexterity can drop, and cold stress adds to fatigue
    Show the answer

    Answer: Her dexterity can drop, and cold stress adds to fatigue

    Why: The TC AIM says cold can impair pilots and ground crews who are not dressed properly: a pilot's dexterity can decrease significantly, and cold temperature stress can add to other stress, such as that caused by fatigue. Cold does not offset fatigue; it adds to it. The TC AIM names the pilot's own dexterity as affected, not just the equipment. Cold stress adds to fatigue rather than replacing it.

    Reference: TC AIM RPA 3.2.22.8 · Level: Basic and Advanced

Meteorology

  1. The report reads: METAR CYOW 101600Z 27010KT 15SM FEW012 SCT035 BKN060 OVC110 18/07 A3001 RMK CU1SC2AC3AC2 SLP163. What is the ceiling?

    • A. 1 200 ft
    • B. 3 500 ft
    • C. 11 000 ft
    • D. 6 000 ft
    Show the answer

    Answer: 6 000 ft

    Why: TC AIM MET 8.2: a ceiling exists at the height of the first layer reported as BKN or OVC, and METAR heights are above the station; here that is BKN060, 6 000 ft AGL. The FEW layer at 1 200 ft is not a ceiling. Nor is the SCT layer at 3 500 ft. The OVC layer at 11 000 ft is above the first broken layer.

    Reference: TC AIM MET 8.2, MET 8.2 (Table 8.2) · Level: Advanced

  2. A crew plans a 2100Z flight near an aerodrome whose TAF includes PROB30 1020/1023 TSRA. What does this group forecast?

    • A. Thunderstorms on 30% of the area, 2000Z to 2300Z
    • B. A 30% chance of thunderstorms from 2000Z to 2300Z
    • C. A 30% chance of thunderstorms from 1020Z to 1023Z
    • D. Thunderstorms on 30% of the area, 1020Z to 1023Z
    Show the answer

    Answer: A 30% chance of thunderstorms from 2000Z to 2300Z

    Why: TC AIM MET 7.3: PROB30 means a 30% probability that the stated conditions occur within the period, and in 1020/1023 the first two digits of each group are the date and the last two the UTC hour, so 2000Z to 2300Z on the 10th; TSRA is a thunderstorm with rain. A share of the area is how GFA spatial qualifiers such as ISOL and OCNL work, not PROB. Reading 1020 and 1023 as times of day ignores the date digits.

    Reference: TC AIM MET 7.3, MET 8.2, MET 4.11 · Level: Advanced

  3. Wet snow has stuck to an RPA during a winter job. The crew carries it into a heated trailer to melt the snow and plans to fly again right after. What does the TC AIM advise before the aircraft goes back out into the cold?

    • A. Launch while it is still warm, before the meltwater can cool
    • B. Wipe the propellers; water on the frame will blow off in flight
    • C. Coat it with automotive de-icing fluid before it goes back out
    • D. Make sure it is completely dry, with no pooled water left
    Show the answer

    Answer: Make sure it is completely dry, with no pooled water left

    Why: The TC AIM says that when an aircraft is put in a heated hangar to remove snow or ice, it must be completely dry when moved outside, otherwise pooled water may refreeze in critical areas or on critical surfaces. Launching while it is warm leaves that water on the aircraft to freeze. Water left on the frame can refreeze where it has pooled rather than blow off. The TC AIM says de-icing and anti-icing fluids should not be used unless the aircraft manufacturer has approved them.

    Reference: TC AIM AIR 2.12.2 · Level: Basic and Advanced

  4. A pilot authorized to fly in a city plans a flight along a downtown street lined with towers on a breezy afternoon. What changes in the wind does the TC AIM say tall buildings and structures can cause in this setting?

    • A. Winds lighter than in the open, with a steady wind direction
    • B. Gusts beyond the RPA's limits, with a steady wind direction
    • C. Gusts beyond the RPA's limits, with shifts in wind direction
    • D. Winds lighter than in the open, with shifts in wind direction
    Show the answer

    Answer: Gusts beyond the RPA's limits, with shifts in wind direction

    Why: The TC AIM warns that tall buildings and structures cause unforeseen changes in wind characteristics, including increased gusts that exceed the RPA's limits and shifts in direction that can blow it off course. Options with a steady wind direction leave out the shifts. Options with winds lighter than in the open contradict the increased gusts the TC AIM names.

    Reference: TC AIM RPA 3.2.22.10 · Level: Basic and Advanced

Navigation

  1. A crew wants an aerodrome sketch and the current status of a small airport 4 NM from a job site. Which NAV CANADA publication does the TC AIM name for this information?

    • A. The Canada Water Aerodrome Supplement
    • B. The Designated Airspace Handbook (DAH)
    • C. An AIP Canada information circular
    • D. The Canada Flight Supplement (CFS)
    Show the answer

    Answer: The Canada Flight Supplement (CFS)

    Why: The CFS publishes VFR information that is not suitable for depiction on the charts, including the current status of individual airports, the facilities and services available and aerodrome sketches. The CWAS covers water aerodromes. The DAH describes the horizontal and vertical limits of airspace. An aeronautical information circular gives advance notice of major changes to legislation, regulations or procedures.

    Reference: TC AIM MAP 2.5.3, MAP 2.5.4, RPA 3.2.3.1, MAP 2.3 · Level: Basic and Advanced

  2. A pilot planning a week-long survey near an uncontrolled aerodrome wants a NOTAM published about her RPA flights so local pilots know. What does the TC AIM say?

    • A. TC, with NAV CANADA, decides whether one is needed
    • B. She files one whenever she judges it to be useful
    • C. She and the aerodrome operator settle it between them
    • D. One is needed for flights within 3 NM of an aerodrome
    Show the answer

    Answer: TC, with NAV CANADA, decides whether one is needed

    Why: The TC AIM says RPAS operations that comply with the CARs or an SFOC-RPAS should not normally need a NOTAM, and that the need to publish one is determined by TC in conjunction with NAV CANADA air traffic services, not left to the RPA pilot's discretion or negotiated between the aerodrome operator, the pilot or other parties. The 3 NM figure is the distance from an airport that applies to basic operations, not a NOTAM requirement.

    Reference: TC AIM RPA 3.2.39.2, RPA 3.3.1 · Level: Basic and Advanced

  3. While reviewing a VNC for a summer job, a pilot notices a soaring symbol a short distance from the site. What does the TC AIM say this kind of symbol marks?

    • A. Regular, significant hang glider or glider activity
    • B. Class F restricted airspace that bars RPA flights
    • C. Airspace where RPA flights first need a NOTAM
    • D. A glider aerodrome certified as an airport
    Show the answer

    Answer: Regular, significant hang glider or glider activity

    Why: The TC AIM says areas with regular, significant hang glider or glider operations are typically marked on VFR aeronautical charts by a hang gliding or soaring symbol, and that hang gliding sites are mostly shared with paragliders. Restricted airspace is denoted CYR, the symbol imposes no NOTAM requirement, and it does not indicate a certified airport.

    Reference: TC AIM AIR 4.15.3, RPA 3.2.3.2 · Level: Basic and Advanced

Flight operations

  1. A client asks for close-up footage of a caribou herd on the tundra. In the interest of conserving wildlife, below what altitude does the TC AIM say pilots must not fly near herds of wild animals?

    • A. 1 000 ft AGL
    • B. 500 ft AGL
    • C. 2 500 ft AGL
    • D. 2 000 ft AGL
    Show the answer

    Answer: 2 000 ft AGL

    Why: In the interest of conserving wildlife, pilots must not fly at an altitude of less than 2 000 ft AGL when in the vicinity of herds of wildlife animals or above wildlife refuges and bird sanctuaries shown on aeronautical charts (RAC 1.10.2); low flying over herds can leave exhausted, disorganized animals open to attack by wolves. 1 000 ft and 500 ft understate the guidance. 2 500 ft is part of the 2 500 to 3 000 ft band in which migrating birds are often seen (AIR 2.4.1), not the conservation figure.

    Reference: TC AIM RAC 1.10.2, AIR 2.4.1 · Level: Basic and Advanced

  2. An RPA hovering on a 40 m tether will light up a work area at night. Under the TC AIM's best practice for tethered operations, what is the minimum distance from people, measured from where the tether is attached to the ground?

    • A. More than 40 m
    • B. More than 70 m
    • C. More than 30 m
    • D. More than 45 m
    Show the answer

    Answer: More than 45 m

    Why: Best practice is to keep tethered operations no closer to people than the tether length plus at least 5 m, measured laterally from the point where the tether is attached to the ground, and to leave room for debris thrown after a crash (RPA 3.2.38): 40 m + 5 m = more than 45 m. The tether length alone leaves no margin. Adding 30 m, or using 30 m alone, borrows a figure from a different rule instead of the tether method.

    Reference: TC AIM RPA 3.2.38 · Level: Basic and Advanced

Theory of flight

  1. A fixed-wing aircraft picks up ice on its wings in flight. Why does the TC AIM warn that it may stall before its stall warning system activates?

    • A. The ice adds weight but leaves the stall speed unchanged
    • B. The ice raises the angle of attack at which the wing stalls
    • C. The ice lowers the angle of attack at which the wing stalls
    • D. The ice leaves the stalling angle of attack as it was
    Show the answer

    Answer: The ice lowers the angle of attack at which the wing stalls

    Why: TC AIM AIR 2.12.3 states that ice on lifting surfaces changes their aerodynamic properties, reducing lift and increasing drag and weight, with a resultant increase in stalling speed and a reduction in the stalling angle of attack; therefore an aerodynamic stall can occur before the stall warning systems activate. A higher stalling angle reverses the effect, and an unchanged stalling angle ignores the reduction the TC AIM gives. Ice does add weight, but the TC AIM says the stalling speed increases rather than staying the same.

    Reference: TC AIM AIR 2.12.3 · Level: Basic and Advanced

Radiotelephony

  1. An RPA crew monitoring an MF hears an arriving pilot tell the FSS, 'WE HAVE THE NUMBERS.' What does the pilot mean?

    • A. The pilot has noted the registration numbers of nearby traffic
    • B. The pilot already has the runway, wind and altimeter information
    • C. The pilot has the frequencies needed for the rest of the trip
    • D. The pilot knows the landing sequence number assigned by ATS
    Show the answer

    Answer: The pilot already has the runway, wind and altimeter information

    Why: TC AIM RAC 4.5.6 encourages pilots to use 'HAVE NUMBERS' on the initial call to a ground station to show that they have received runway, wind and altimeter information from the previous aerodrome advisory, which reduces frequency congestion. It does not refer to registrations or frequencies. A landing sequence number is what a tower gives with 'NUMBER' (RAC 4.4.2), not what this phrase reports.

    Reference: TC AIM RAC 4.5.6, RAC 4.4.2 · Level: Advanced
