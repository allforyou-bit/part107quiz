How to Read a Sectional Chart: A Drone Pilot's Guide

Updated August 2026 · Part 107 Quiz editorial team

On exam day you will be handed a booklet of chart excerpts — the FAA's Airman Knowledge Testing Supplement — and asked questions that can only be answered by reading them. This is the single most reliable place to lose points, because chart questions do not reward familiarity with the regulations. They reward knowing what a fuzzy magenta band means and being able to subtract one number from another under time pressure.

The good news is that the sectional is a closed system. There are maybe fifteen symbols a remote pilot ever needs, and once you can name them the questions become arithmetic. This guide covers those fifteen.

What a Sectional Chart Is

A VFR Sectional Aeronautical Chart is the FAA's 1:500,000-scale visual navigation chart — roughly one inch to seven nautical miles. Each chart covers a named region (Seattle, Dallas–Ft Worth, New York), and the FAA publishes them on a 56-day cycle. Every chart carries an effective date on its cover panel, and an expired chart is a genuine hazard: airspace boundaries, tower heights, and frequencies all change between editions.

You are not expected to buy paper charts. SkyVector, VFRMap, and the FAA's own digital products display the same charts free, and B4UFLY and the LAANC supplier apps overlay the airspace data on top of a map. But the exam tests the chart itself, in black-and-white-and-magenta, so learn it in that form.

Distances on a sectional are nautical miles, and every altitude printed on the chart is MSL unless it appears in parentheses. Your Part 107 limits are AGL. Almost every chart question on the exam lives in the gap between those two facts.

Airspace: The Color Code

Two rules cover almost every case. Solid or dashed lines mean the airspace reaches the ground, so a drone flight inside them needs authorization no matter how low it stays. Fuzzy vignettes mean the airspace starts overhead — 700 ft AGL for magenta, 1,200 ft for blue — so a flight capped at 400 ft AGL underneath is in Class G and needs nothing.

The dashed magenta line versus the fuzzy magenta band is the distinction the exam returns to again and again, and the two look similar at a glance on a small excerpt. Dashed line, sharp edge, authorization required. Fuzzy band, soft edge, you are underneath it. Our airspace classes guide goes deeper on what each class is for, and the LAANC guide covers how the authorization itself gets granted.

Reading the numbers inside Class B and C

Class B and Class C segments carry a pair of numbers stacked like a fraction — for example 100 over 40. Those are hundreds of feet MSL: the shelf runs from 4,000 ft MSL up to 10,000 ft MSL. A floor written as SFC means the segment reaches the surface. Class D airspace instead shows a single bracketed number such as [25], which is the ceiling in hundreds of feet MSL — 2,500 ft MSL, with the floor at the surface.

Airport Symbols and Data Blocks

Airport color tells you one thing immediately: blue means the field has a control tower, magenta means it does not. Airports with a hard-surfaced runway of 8,069 ft or longer are drawn as a scaled runway outline; smaller fields appear as circles with runway lines. Tick marks around the circle indicate fuel is available during normal business hours, and a star above the symbol means a rotating beacon operates from sunset to sunrise.

Underneath the symbol sits the data block. A typical non-towered entry reads something like:

HARTSVILLE (HTV) ⊕ 412 L 42 122.7

  • 412 — field elevation in feet MSL. This is the number you use to convert chart MSL altitudes into your AGL limits.
  • L — runway lighting is available, at least part of the night.
  • 42 — length of the longest runway in hundreds of feet: 4,200 ft.
  • 122.7 — the frequency. A circled Ⓒ beside it marks the CTAF, the common traffic advisory frequency pilots self-announce on at a non-towered field. At a towered field you will instead see CT followed by the tower frequency, and often an ATIS frequency below it.

For a remote pilot the CTAF matters more than it looks. Monitoring it near an uncontrolled field is how you hear the Cessna announcing a left downwind before you see it — and yielding right of way to manned aircraft is not optional under 107.37.

Terrain, Obstructions, and the MSL Trap

Each half-degree quadrangle on the chart carries a large bold blue number called the Maximum Elevation Figure (MEF). It is written in thousands and hundreds of feet MSL, so an MEF shown as a large 2 and a smaller 1 means 2,100 ft MSL. The MEF is the highest known terrain or obstacle anywhere in that quadrangle, rounded up — a quick clearance check, not a precise obstacle height.

Individual obstructions get their own symbols, and they come with two numbers:

  • Bold number, no parentheses — the top of the obstruction in feet MSL.
  • Number in parentheses — the height of the obstruction above the ground, in feet AGL.
  • Lightning-bolt rays around the symbol mean the obstruction is lighted.
  • A taller, doubled symbol marks obstructions 1,000 ft AGL or higher.

So a tower printed as 1179 (285) stands 285 ft above the ground, and its top is 1,179 ft above sea level — which also tells you the ground beneath it sits at roughly 894 ft MSL.

The conversion the exam wants. A chart gives you MSL. Part 107 gives you AGL. To find your own MSL ceiling at a site, add 400 to the ground elevation: launching from a 1,200 ft MSL field, your 400 ft AGL ceiling is 1,600 ft MSL. To find how far below an obstruction top you would be flying, subtract. Practice the subtraction until it is automatic — under exam pressure this is where careless points go.

Remember the one structure exception in 107.51(b): within a 400-ft radius of a structure, you may climb to 400 ft above that structure's uppermost limit. A 285-ft tower therefore supports a legal 685 ft AGL inspection flight — but only while you stay inside that radius.

Special Use Airspace

Hatched bands mark airspace with restrictions attached. The name inside tells you which kind, and the chart margin carries a panel listing each area's altitudes, operating times, and controlling agency.

TypePrefixWhat it means for you
ProhibitedP-No flight, ever. Usually security-driven.
RestrictedR-Hazards such as artillery or guided missiles. Entry needs permission from the controlling agency.
WarningW-Same hazards, over international waters.
Military Operations AreaMOAMilitary training. Not prohibited, but expect fast, maneuvering traffic.
AlertA-High volume of training activity. Flight is permitted; vigilance is expected.

National security UAS flight restrictions and TFRs are a separate matter. They are not printed on the sectional at all, because they change daily — check NOTAMs or B4UFLY before every flight, even after LAANC has approved you.

A Worked Example

Say a chart excerpt shows your job site inside a dashed magenta circle around a magenta airport symbol whose data block reads 318 L 35 123.0 Ⓒ, with a tower half a mile east printed as 703 (385).

  1. Airspace. Dashed magenta means Class E to the surface, so this flight needs FAA authorization before takeoff. The magenta airport symbol means there is no control tower — the authorization still comes from the FAA, not from anyone at the field.
  2. Ceiling. Field elevation 318 ft MSL, so a 400 ft AGL flight tops out near 718 ft MSL.
  3. The tower. It rises 385 ft AGL, above your 400-ft working ceiling in practical terms, and its top at 703 ft MSL sits just below your ceiling in MSL. Stay well clear laterally, or use the 107.51(b) structure exception deliberately and stay inside the 400-ft radius.
  4. Radio. 123.0 is the CTAF. Monitoring it costs nothing and tells you when someone is entering the pattern.

Four steps, no memorized regulation beyond the airspace table. That is what chart questions actually are.

What the Exam Expects

You will not be asked to plot a course or to use a plotter. Remote pilot chart questions cluster tightly around: identifying the airspace class at a marked point, deciding whether authorization is required there, reading a field elevation or a runway length out of a data block, converting between MSL and AGL, identifying the CTAF, and interpreting an obstruction's two heights. The legend is printed in the supplement you are given, so the skill being tested is speed and confidence, not recall.

FAQ

Do I get a sectional chart during the Part 107 exam?

Yes. The testing center supplies the FAA Airman Knowledge Testing Supplement, which contains the chart excerpts and the full legend the questions refer to. You do not need to memorize the symbols, but you do need to find them fast — the clock is the real constraint.

What is the difference between the dashed magenta line and the fuzzy magenta band?

The dashed line is Class E beginning at the surface, so every flight inside it requires FAA authorization. The fuzzy band is Class E beginning at 700 ft AGL, which sits entirely above the Part 107 ceiling — a flight at or below 400 ft AGL underneath it is in Class G and needs no authorization.

Are altitudes on a sectional MSL or AGL?

MSL, with one exception: obstruction heights printed in parentheses are AGL. Part 107's 400-ft ceiling is AGL, so convert by adding the ground or field elevation. Mixing the two is the most common chart error on the exam.

How often are sectional charts updated?

The FAA publishes VFR sectionals on a 56-day cycle, and each chart shows its effective date. Digital chart viewers and LAANC supplier apps pull the current edition automatically, but if you keep a paper chart in the vehicle, check the date before you rely on it. Always confirm current requirements at faa.gov.

Are TFRs shown on the sectional?

No. Temporary flight restrictions are issued and lifted far faster than the chart cycle, so they never appear on a printed sectional. Check NOTAMs or B4UFLY before each flight — a LAANC authorization does not override an active TFR.
