24 free practice questions, with answers and references

Pick an answer, then open “Show the answer”. These 24 questions are original practice questions, not actual FAA test questions.

Area I · Preflight Preparation

  1. An airplane's weight and balance record gives a weight that includes the airframe, engine, fixed ballast, unusable fuel, full engine oil, and the optional equipment installed in that airplane. Which term from the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge names this weight?

    • A. Basic empty weight
    • B. Standard empty weight
    • C. Licensed empty weight
    Show the answer

    Answer: Basic empty weight

    Why: The Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge defines basic empty weight as the standard empty weight plus the weight of optional and special equipment that has been installed; standard empty weight already includes fixed ballast, unusable fuel and full engine oil. Standard empty weight alone leaves out the optional equipment. Licensed empty weight, an older term, counts only undrainable oil, not full engine oil.

    ACS PA.I.F.K2f · Reference: PHAK ch 10, Terms and Definitions

  2. An airplane has Fowler flaps, which slide back on tracks and then deflect downward. How do the first and last portions of their travel differ?

    • A. The first part adds mostly drag; the last part adds much lift for little drag
    • B. Each part adds lift and drag in about the same proportion as the others
    • C. The first part adds much lift for little drag; the last part adds mostly drag
    Show the answer

    Answer: The first part adds much lift for little drag; the last part adds mostly drag

    Why: The Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge says a Fowler flap increases lift a great deal with very little drag in the first portion of its extension, as it adds both wing area and camber, and that in the last portion of its travel it adds drag with little additional lift. The reverse order puts the drag first. The portions do not add lift and drag in the same proportion; the handbook contrasts them.

    ACS PA.I.G.K1b · Reference: PHAK ch 6, Flaps

  3. Climbing through a cloud where the outside air temperature is -5°C, an airplane meets large supercooled water droplets. Which type of structural ice is most likely, according to the Aviation Weather Handbook?

    • A. Clear ice, formed as the drops freeze slowly
    • B. Rime ice, formed as the drops freeze instantly
    • C. Mixed ice, built up in layers of rime and clear
    Show the answer

    Answer: Clear ice, formed as the drops freeze slowly

    Why: The Aviation Weather Handbook says clear (glaze) ice forms by the relatively slow freezing of large supercooled droplets, more often with warmer temperatures and higher liquid water content, and that in general clear ice occurs when the temperature is warmer than -10°C. Rime ice comes from the instant freezing of small droplets and favors colder temperatures, generally colder than -15°C. Mixed ice, a mixture of clear and rime that shows as layers, generally forms at temperatures between those two, not with large drops at -5°C.

    ACS PA.I.C.K3i · Reference: AWH ch 20, 20.3.2 Clear Icing; AWH ch 20, 20.3.4 Icing Factors; AWH ch 20, 20.3.1 Rime Icing; AWH ch 20, 20.3.3 Mixed Icing

  4. In daylight, a military jet takes position slightly above and ahead of a private pilot's airplane, on its left side, rocks its wings, and then makes a slow level turn to the left. What is the correct response?

    • A. Rock the wings and follow the intercepting aircraft
    • B. Rock the wings and continue on the present course
    • C. Lower the landing gear and prepare to land at once
    Show the answer

    Answer: Rock the wings and follow the intercepting aircraft

    Why: The AIM's table of intercepting signals gives rocking wings from slightly above, ahead of and to the left of the intercepted aircraft, followed by a slow level turn, as "You have been intercepted. Follow me"; the airplane's daytime response is rocking wings and following. Continuing on course is the response only after the interceptor signals "You may proceed" with an abrupt climbing break-away turn of 90 degrees or more. Lowering the gear is the response to a different signal, the interceptor circling the aerodrome, lowering its gear and overflying the runway, which means land at this aerodrome.

    ACS PA.I.D.K6 · Reference: AIM 5-6-14

  5. According to the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge, how does a pilot's performance generally change as stress builds?

    • A. It declines steadily from the first sign of stress
    • B. It holds level until stress is severe, then drops
    • C. It rises at first, peaks, then falls off rapidly
    Show the answer

    Answer: It rises at first, peaks, then falls off rapidly

    Why: The Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge says performance generally increases with the onset of stress, peaks, and then falls off rapidly as stress exceeds a person's ability to cope, and that a certain amount of stress is good because it keeps a person alert and prevents complacency. A steady decline from the first stress ignores that early gain. Performance does not hold level either; it improves before it falls.

    ACS PA.I.H.K1g · Reference: PHAK ch 2, Stress Management

Area II · Preflight Procedures

  1. A pilot has cranked an engine continuously for 30 seconds without a start, and the POH gives no starter limit of its own. Following the Airplane Flying Handbook, what should come before the next cranking attempt?

    • A. A pause of about 5 seconds to let the engine settle
    • B. A starter cool-down of at least 30 seconds to 1 minute
    • C. None, since the starter is built for continuous duty
    Show the answer

    Answer: A starter cool-down of at least 30 seconds to 1 minute

    Why: The Airplane Flying Handbook says to avoid continuous starter operation for more than 30 seconds without a cool-down period of at least 30 seconds to 1 minute, because these electric motors are not designed for continuous duty and heat buildup can damage internal starter components. A pause of a few seconds is far short of that cool-down, and a starter built for continuous duty is exactly what the handbook says these motors are not.

    ACS PA.II.C.K3 · Reference: AFH ch 2, Engine Starting

  2. Holding short of the runway and facing a strong, steady wind, a pilot sees the airspeed indicator showing about 15 knots with the airplane stopped. The altimeter and vertical speed indicator show nothing unusual. According to the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge, how should the pilot treat the reading?

    • A. Accept it, then check that airspeed builds on the roll
    • B. Taxi back and have a mechanic inspect the pitot tube
    • C. Take off, treating each airspeed reading as 15 knots high
    Show the answer

    Answer: Accept it, then check that airspeed builds on the roll

    Why: The Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge says the airspeed indicator should read zero before takeoff, but a strong wind blowing directly into the pitot tube may make it read higher than zero, and when beginning the takeoff the pilot makes sure the airspeed is increasing at an appropriate rate. A reading explained by the wind is not a fault that calls for a mechanic, and it is not a fixed instrument error to subtract from later readings.

    ACS PA.II.F.K1b · Reference: PHAK ch 8, Instrument Check

  3. Checking the left tank's first fuel drain during preflight, a pilot sees clear, bead-like droplets resting at the bottom of the cup. According to the Airplane Flying Handbook, what should the pilot do next?

    • A. Discard it and fly; the water is now drained
    • B. Ground the airplane until a mechanic drains it
    • C. Keep taking samples until no water appears
    Show the answer

    Answer: Keep taking samples until no water appears

    Why: The Airplane Flying Handbook says that if water is found in the first fuel sample, sampling should continue until no water and contamination appears; water usually shows as bubble- or bead-like droplets at the bottom of the sample. One drained sample does not show that the water is gone, and the handbook warns that in extreme cases the entire sample may be water. Further investigation by qualified maintenance personnel is the step for significant or consistent water or contamination, not the first response to one sample.

    ACS PA.II.A.K3c · Reference: AFH ch 2, Fuel and Oil

Area III · Airport and Seaplane Base Operations

  1. A VFR pilot with an operating Mode C transponder is receiving radar traffic advisories at night over hilly terrain and wants ATC's automated low-altitude warning to cover the flight. What does the AIM say is needed?

    • A. An IFR flight plan filed and activated with ATC
    • B. A request to the controller for MSAW monitoring
    • C. Nothing beyond the radar advisories already received
    Show the answer

    Answer: A request to the controller for MSAW monitoring

    Why: The AIM says that, when MSAW is operating, the radar facility monitors aircraft with an operating Mode C transponder that are tracked by the system and are either on an IFR flight plan or operating VFR and have requested MSAW monitoring; VFR pilots with Mode C may request it. An IFR flight plan is one way to be covered, not the only one. Receiving traffic advisories does not by itself bring a VFR flight under MSAW monitoring; the pilot must ask for it.

    ACS PA.III.A.K7 · Reference: AIM 4-1-16

  2. Three airliners of the same type fly past the airport in turn. Other factors being equal, which leaves the strongest wingtip vortices?

    • A. One at high weight, flaps up, flying slowly
    • B. One at high weight, flaps down, flying faster
    • C. One at low weight, flaps down, flying slowly
    Show the answer

    Answer: One at high weight, flaps up, flying slowly

    Why: The AIM says vortex strength increases with operating weight and with decreasing speed, that extending flaps changes the vortex characteristics, and that because a "dirty" configuration hastens wake decay, the greatest vortex strength occurs when the generating aircraft is heavy, clean, and slow. Flaps down and a higher speed both weaken the wake. A lower weight gives weaker vortices, since strength rises in proportion to operating weight.

    ACS PA.III.B.R3 · Reference: AIM 7-4-3

Area IV · Takeoffs, Landings, and Go-Arounds

  1. A pilot is about to land on Runway 36 shortly after a large airplane landed on it. With which reported surface wind does the AIM call for maximum caution about that airplane's wake?

    • A. Wind 360 at 5 knots
    • B. Wind 140 at 5 knots
    • C. Wind 270 at 20 knots
    Show the answer

    Answer: Wind 140 at 5 knots

    Why: Landing on Runway 36 means landing toward the north, so a wind from 140 at 5 knots blows from behind and to the right of the airplane: a light quartering tailwind. The AIM says a tailwind can move the preceding aircraft's vortices forward into the touchdown zone and that the light quartering tailwind requires maximum caution. Wind 360 at 5 knots is a light headwind straight down the runway, and 270 at 20 knots is a strong direct crosswind; the AIM does not single out either for maximum caution.

    ACS PA.IV.B.R2d · Reference: AIM 7-4-4

  2. A POH limits how long a forward slip may be held. According to the Airplane Flying Handbook, what is a limit like this meant to prevent?

    • A. A tail stall from changed airflow with flaps extended
    • B. Fuel starvation from fuel forced to one side of a tank
    • C. Misleading fuel-gauge readings that hide low fuel
    Show the answer

    Answer: Fuel starvation from fuel forced to one side of a tank

    Why: The Airplane Flying Handbook says some airplanes limit slips in duration or by fuel quantity to preclude fuel starvation caused when fuel is forced to one side of a tank in uncoordinated flight. Recommendations or limitations on slips with flaps extended are a separate matter that exists for aerodynamic reasons. Fuel-gauge accuracy is not what these limits address.

    ACS PA.IV.M.R7 · Reference: AFH ch 9, Intentional Slips

  3. On a normal takeoff, a student pulls the control wheel firmly aft well before the airplane reaches its rotation speed. According to the Airplane Flying Handbook, what may result?

    • A. The airplane may settle back to the runway or even stall
    • B. The airplane lifts off sooner, shortening the takeoff distance
    • C. The airplane accelerates faster once the nosewheel is off
    Show the answer

    Answer: The airplane may settle back to the runway or even stall

    Why: The Airplane Flying Handbook warns that forcing the airplane off with too much back-elevator pressure before adequate flying speed can make the wing's angle of attack excessive, so the airplane settles back to the runway or even stalls. Rather than shortening the takeoff, excessive back pressure gives an excessively high pitch attitude and may delay it. Faster acceleration does not follow either; the handbook says the result may be a delayed takeoff.

    ACS PA.IV.A.R5 · Reference: AFH ch 6, Lift-Off

  4. A pilot of a retractable-gear airplane takes off from a strip covered with wet snow and slush. What does the Airplane Flying Handbook advise about the landing gear after lift-off?

    • A. Retract it at once, ahead of the usual retraction point
    • B. Delay retraction for a time past the usual retraction point
    • C. Retract it at the usual point, once a positive climb is shown
    Show the answer

    Answer: Delay retraction for a time past the usual retraction point

    Why: The Airplane Flying Handbook says that when departing from an airstrip with wet snow or slush on the takeoff surface, the gear should not be retracted immediately, so that any wet snow or slush can be air-dried. Retracting at once, for example to keep slush from freezing in the airflow, is what the handbook says not to do in these conditions. Retracting at the usual point gives the slush no time to dry; the handbook makes the slush case an exception to its usual retraction.

    ACS PA.IV.C.R2e · Reference: AFH ch 6, Soft-Field Initial Climb

  5. A pilot has accepted a land and hold short clearance and is on short final when a go-around becomes necessary. Which statement matches the AIM?

    • A. The pilot needs an amended clearance from ATC before going around
    • B. The pilot may go around, keeping clear of traffic and promptly telling ATC
    • C. The pilot may go around and need not tell ATC until back in the pattern
    Show the answer

    Answer: The pilot may go around, keeping clear of traffic and promptly telling ATC

    Why: The AIM says a LAHSO clearance does not preclude a rejected landing, and that a pilot who must reject a landing after accepting one should maintain safe separation from other aircraft or vehicles and promptly notify the controller. No amended clearance is needed first; the AIM states the clearance does not preclude a rejected landing. Waiting until back in the pattern to tell ATC conflicts with the AIM's call to notify the controller promptly.

    ACS PA.IV.N.R9 · Reference: AIM 4-3-11

Area V · Performance Maneuvers and Ground Reference Maneuvers

  1. Entering a steep turn at the recommended entry speed, a pilot rolls toward a 50-degree bank. According to the Airplane Flying Handbook, when should elevator back pressure and added power generally come in?

    • A. Once the full 50 degrees of bank is reached
    • B. During the roll-in, before passing about 30 degrees
    • C. Before the roll-in begins, while the wings are still level
    Show the answer

    Answer: During the roll-in, before passing about 30 degrees

    Why: The Airplane Flying Handbook says that as the bank angle is being established, generally prior to 30 degrees of bank, elevator back pressure should be smoothly applied to increase the angle of attack and power should be added. The extra back pressure is needed because banking divides the total lift into vertical and horizontal components. Waiting until the full bank is set leaves the vertical component short during the whole roll-in, so altitude is lost. With the wings still level no lift has yet been diverted into the turn, so the handbook ties the added pressure and power to the bank as it builds, not to the moment before the roll-in.

    ACS PA.V.A.K1 · Reference: AFH ch 10, Steep Turns

Area VI · Navigation

  1. An older airplane's airspeed indicator is marked in miles per hour, and the pilot computes a true airspeed of 115 mph. The forecast gives a headwind component of 20 knots. What groundspeed should the pilot use, in knots? (1 knot = 1.15 mph.)

    • A. 95 knots
    • B. 80 knots
    • C. 92 knots
    Show the answer

    Answer: 80 knots

    Why: The Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge converts knots to miles per hour by multiplying by 1.15, so 115 mph ÷ 1.15 = 100 knots, and 100 - 20 = 80 knots. 95 knots subtracts knots from miles per hour without converting. 92 knots is the correct groundspeed in miles per hour (115 - 23) labeled as knots.

    ACS PA.VI.A.K5c · Reference: PHAK ch 16, Converting Knots to Miles Per Hour

  2. A VFR pilot who is unsure of the airplane's position wants vectors to the nearest airport from an FAA air traffic control facility. According to the AIM, which conditions must be met for the facility to provide this navigation service?

    • A. Radio contact, Mode C altitude, and a filed flight plan
    • B. Radio contact, radar coverage, and radar identification
    • C. Radar coverage, radar identification, and Mode C altitude
    Show the answer

    Answer: Radio contact, radar coverage, and radar identification

    Why: The AIM says radar-equipped ATC facilities provide radar assistance and navigation service (vectors) to VFR aircraft provided the aircraft can communicate with the facility, is within radar coverage, and can be radar identified. A filed flight plan and Mode C altitude reporting are not among those conditions. Radar coverage and identification are not enough without radio contact, because the aircraft must be able to communicate with the facility.

    ACS PA.VI.D.K2 · Reference: AIM 4-1-17; PHAK ch 16, Lost Procedures

Area VII · Slow Flight and Stalls

  1. Before practicing slow flight in the landing configuration, a learner asks how to find the target airspeed for this maneuver in this particular airplane. Which method does the Airplane Flying Handbook describe?

    • A. Reduce speed to the stall warning, lower the nose until it stops, add power to hold altitude
    • B. Hold altitude while reducing speed until the stall warning sounds, then keep that speed with power
    • C. Multiply the published stall speed for that configuration by 1.3, then fly that speed level
    Show the answer

    Answer: Reduce speed to the stall warning, lower the nose until it stops, add power to hold altitude

    Why: The Airplane Flying Handbook gives one way to find the target: slow to the stall warning in the desired configuration, pitch the nose down slightly to eliminate the warning, add power to maintain altitude, and note the airspeed. Holding the speed at which the warning sounds is flight with the stall warning active, while the handbook defines slow flight as controlled flight without activating the stall warning. A 1.3 multiple of the stall speed is not the handbook's method for slow flight; the handbook puts the slow flight target just above the stall warning, approximately 5 to 10 knots above the stall speed for that condition.

    ACS PA.VII.A.K1 · Reference: AFH ch 5, Slow Flight; AFH ch 5, Performing the Slow Flight Maneuver

  2. On a checkride, the examiner asks for a full stall rather than an impending one. According to the Airplane Flying Handbook, which indication is typical of a full stall rather than an impending one?

    • A. A stall warning horn that has just begun to sound
    • B. A nose-down pitch the pilot cannot readily stop
    • C. Buffeting felt through the airframe and yoke
    Show the answer

    Answer: A nose-down pitch the pilot cannot readily stop

    Why: The Airplane Flying Handbook says the indications of a full stall are typically an uncommanded nose-down pitch that cannot be readily arrested, possibly with an uncommanded roll. A stall warning that has just begun to sound marks an impending stall, which the handbook defines as the angle of attack causing a stall warning before the critical angle is reached. Buffeting is likewise listed as an indication of an impending stall, not proof that the critical angle has been exceeded.

    ACS PA.VII.B.K2 · Reference: AFH ch 5, Stalls

Area VIII · Basic Instrument Maneuvers

  1. A pilot is 20° from a desired VOR course and selects an intercept heading. Following the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge, which angle of intercept should the pilot choose?

    • A. 15 degrees
    • B. 45 degrees
    • C. 110 degrees
    Show the answer

    Answer: 45 degrees

    Why: The Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge says the angle of intercept must be greater than the degrees from course but should not exceed 90°, so 45° fits a position 20° from course. 15° is less than the 20° the airplane is off course. 110° exceeds the 90° limit.

    ACS PA.VIII.F.K2 · Reference: PHAK ch 16, Angle of Intercept

Area IX · Emergency Operations

  1. A pilot is gliding toward a field for a forced landing in an airplane with electrically operated flaps. According to the Airplane Flying Handbook, when should the battery master switch be turned off?

    • A. As soon as the engine quits, while up high
    • B. Before touchdown, once nothing else needs it
    • C. After the airplane has come to a stop
    Show the answer

    Answer: Before touchdown, once nothing else needs it

    Why: The Airplane Flying Handbook says deactivating the electrical system before touchdown reduces the chance of a post-crash fire, but the battery master switch should not be turned off until the pilot no longer needs electrical power for vital systems, which here include the flaps. Turning it off as soon as the engine quits removes power the pilot still needs for the flaps and radios. Waiting until the airplane stops gives up the fire-risk reduction the handbook gets from switching off before touchdown.

    ACS PA.IX.B.R4 · Reference: AFH ch 18, Airplane Configuration

Area XI · Night Operations

  1. On final to a small airport, a pilot using a pulsating visual approach slope indicator sees a pulsating red light. What does it indicate?

    • A. The airplane is slightly below the glide path
    • B. The airplane is above the desired glide path
    • C. The airplane is further below the glide path
    Show the answer

    Answer: The airplane is further below the glide path

    Why: On a pulsating VASI, slightly below the glide path shows a steady red light; if the aircraft descends further below the glide path, the red light starts to pulsate. Above the glide path is shown by a pulsating white light.

    ACS PA.XI.A.K2 · Reference: AIM 2-1-2

Area XII · Postflight Procedures

  1. Securing an airplane for the night, a pilot finds chains at one tie-down spot and ropes at another. According to the Airplane Flying Handbook, how tight should each be?

    • A. Chains pulled taut; ropes left with some slack
    • B. Chains left with some slack; ropes cinched snugly
    • C. Both chains and ropes pulled as tight as can be
    Show the answer

    Answer: Chains left with some slack; ropes cinched snugly

    Why: The Airplane Flying Handbook says chains are not flexible and should not be made taut, so the airplane can move a little without structural damage, while flexible ropes may be reasonably cinched to the tie-down rings. Pulling the chains taut and leaving the ropes loose reverses the handbook's guidance. Pulling both as tight as possible ignores the warning that taut chains can damage the airframe.

    ACS PA.XII.A.K1 · Reference: AFH ch 2, Securing and Servicing
