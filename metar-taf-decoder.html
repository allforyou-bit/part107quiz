How to read a METAR, group by group

A METAR always keeps the same order. When an element is not reported, it is simply left out (FAA AIM 7-1-29):

GroupExampleWhat it means
TypeMETAR / SPECIRoutine hourly observation, or a special one issued between them.
StationKPITFour-letter ICAO identifier. In the contiguous 48 states it is K plus the three-letter identifier.
Date and time091955ZDay of the month (09) and time (1955) in UTC.
ModifierAUTO / CORAUTO: fully automated, no human. COR: a corrected report.
Wind22015G25KTFrom 220° true at 15 knots, gusts to 25. VRB = variable, 00000KT = calm.
Visibility3/4SMPrevailing visibility in statute miles and fractions.
Runway visual rangeR28L/2600FTHow far down runway 28L a pilot can see, in feet.
WeatherTSRA, -RA, BRIntensity (- light, none moderate, + heavy), descriptor, then the phenomenon.
SkyOVC010CBCover (FEW, SCT, BKN, OVC), base in hundreds of feet above ground level, and CB or TCU.
Temperature / dew point18/16Degrees Celsius. M means minus: 00/M03.
AltimeterA299229.92 inches of mercury.
RemarksRMK AO2 SLP045Extra detail, such as the station type and sea-level pressure (1004.5 hPa).

Two details trip people up. The wind direction is where the wind comes from, and it is referenced to true north. Cloud heights are above ground level and in hundreds of feet, so BKN008 is 800 feet, not 8 or 8,000.

Ceiling and flight category

A METAR does not label the ceiling. For aviation purposes it is the lowest broken or overcast layer, or the vertical visibility into an obscuration (VV). The FAA groups conditions into four categories (AIM 7-1-7):

  • LIFR: ceiling below 500 ft and/or visibility below 1 mile.
  • IFR: ceiling 500 to below 1,000 ft and/or visibility 1 to below 3 miles.
  • MVFR: ceiling 1,000 to 3,000 ft and/or visibility 3 to 5 miles.
  • VFR: ceiling above 3,000 ft and visibility above 5 miles, including sky clear.

These categories describe conditions for flying in general. They are not Part 107 rules; the Part 107 limits are below.

How to read a TAF

A TAF is a forecast for an airport, usually for 24 hours and at some airports for 30. It uses the same codes as a METAR, split into time periods:

  • Valid period, such as 0918/1024: from 1800Z on the 9th to 2400Z on the 10th. In each half, the first two digits are the date and the last two the hour.
  • FM091930: from 1930Z on the 9th, a rapid change to a completely new set of conditions. Read the first two digits as the date. FM100000 is 0000Z on the 10th, not 1000Z.
  • TEMPO 0920/0922: temporary conditions. Each spell generally lasts less than an hour, and together they cover less than half the period.
  • BECMG 1013/1015: a gradual change at some time in that period.
  • PROB30 1004/1007: a 30 to 39 percent chance of the thunderstorms or precipitation that follow (PROB40: 40 to 49 percent).
  • P6SM means visibility more than 6 statute miles. NSW means no significant weather; the other elements carry over.
  • WS010/31022KT: low-level wind shear at 1,000 ft, where the wind is from 310° at 22 knots.

Except in an FM group, a change group lists only the elements that change. Everything else carries over from the line before it.

What a report means under Part 107

Three limits in 14 CFR 107.51 matter when you read a report:

  • Flight visibility of at least 3 statute miles, as observed from your control station.
  • At least 500 feet below clouds and 2,000 feet horizontally from clouds.
  • No higher than 400 feet above ground level, unless you are within 400 feet of a structure and no more than 400 feet above its top.

The report describes the airport, not your site. Visibility is the airport's surface visibility, and cloud heights are above the airport's ground level. With a lowest layer at 700 feet, for example, staying 500 feet below it means flying no higher than 200 feet above that ground level. The decoder shows that number for each report. FEW and SCT layers have gaps: away from those clouds, the rule is to stay 2,000 feet horizontally from them.

Frequently asked questions

Is the wind direction in a METAR true or magnetic?

True. The first three digits of the wind group are the direction the wind is blowing from, rounded to the nearest 10 degrees and referenced to true north (FAA AIM 7-1-29). 22015G25KT is wind from 220 degrees true at 15 knots, gusting to 25.

What is the ceiling in a METAR or TAF?

The lowest broken (BKN) or overcast (OVC) layer, or the vertical visibility (VV) into an obscuration. FEW and SCT layers are not a ceiling. Cloud heights are three digits in hundreds of feet above ground level, so BKN008 is a ceiling of 800 feet.

Is METAR visibility the same as Part 107 flight visibility?

No. A METAR gives the prevailing surface visibility at the airport. Part 107 requires at least 3 statute miles of flight visibility as observed from your control station (14 CFR 107.51(c)). A report under 3 statute miles is a strong sign the minimum will not be met nearby, but the rule is about what you can see from where you fly.

How do I read FM, TEMPO, BECMG and PROB30 in a TAF?

FM plus six digits (date, hour, minute) starts a completely new set of conditions. TEMPO gives temporary conditions, each spell generally under an hour and together less than half of the period. BECMG is a gradual change somewhere in the period. PROB30 and PROB40 give a 30 to 39 or 40 to 49 percent chance of thunderstorms or precipitation. Except for FM, only the elements that change are listed.

What is the difference between SKC and CLR?

SKC means sky clear and is reported by manual stations and used in TAFs. CLR is used by automated stations when no clouds are detected below 12,000 feet, because automated stations cannot report clouds above 12,000 feet.
